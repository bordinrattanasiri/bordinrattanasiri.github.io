One lesson, measured

The explanation was good. It held about a quarter of what I needed, and the chat had no way to show that.

What happened

I teach music theory in Thailand — fundamentals through harmony, counterpoint and form, to students from complete beginners up to university level. Fifteen years of it. My own maths stopped in secondary school.

In July 2026 I asked Claude to teach me derivatives, a topic I had never studied. The explanation it gave in chat was good. Good enough that I asked for it back word for word as a web page, with two buttons on every sub-step: one to mark it understood, and one that opens a box to type a question about that step. Whatever I asked came back as a panel attached to the step itself, so the answer sat where the confusion was. Claude cut its own answer into 25 steps; I did not choose that number, or the places it cut.

Nothing else changed. Same words, same model, same session, a few minutes apart. The only new thing was somewhere to point.

I asked 18 questions. Eleven of them landed on step 2.1. Answering all 18 took three times more writing than the whole original explanation contained.

Step 2.1 was not a hard step. It was missing about four sections' worth of ground, and nothing in the chat version could have revealed that — not to me, and not to the model. A learner cannot name what they do not understand; that is what not understanding is. Given somewhere to point, they can point, and the shape of where they point is the missing curriculum.

Reading down the first eight rows: the explanation had introduced s(t) = t² as a fact. Everything under it — where the formula came from, what a ramp has to do with gravity, what an acceleration unit means, why seconds appear twice — was ground the step stood on and did not lay. That is not a hard step. It is four missing steps wearing one number.

The method

After the lesson I asked Claude to write down what it had learned about teaching me, so that the next subject would not start from zero. That became two skill files and three reference documents. Claude wrote the prose; I decided what belonged in it, and every rule in it exists because its absence cost me something in session 1.

The rule that matters most is the one the chart above taught: a cluster of questions on one step means a section is missing, not that the step is hard. The repair is never a longer answer. It is new steps on the main line.

Beyond this one lesson I can see many more applications. My own subject would need a completely different page — audio, notation, replay — which is exactly the point. The workflow has to be designed per subject, not once. And all of this was built in Thai.
